Should Congress Expand the U.S. House of Representatives?

Since the Permanent Apportionment Act of 1929, the U.S. House has been capped at 435 voting members—leaving each representative responsible for over 760,000 citizens. Share your perspective on constitutional representation, local accountability, and practical governance.

Restoring James Madison's Vision of Local Accountability

Hon. Marcus Vance ✓ Verified Member
Published October 18, 2026
24
In Federalist No. 55, James Madison warned that too few representatives would leave lawmakers detached from the everyday lives and moral convictions of their constituents. Today, one U.S. Representative speaks for more than three-quarters of a million people—making grassroots campaigning nearly impossible without massive donor networks. Expanding the House modestly, perhaps using the "Cube Root Rule," would shrink congressional districts, allow ordinary Christian citizens of modest means to run for office, and restore genuine accountability between representatives and local churches and families.

Fiscal Responsibility and Federal Bureaucracy Concerns

Rebecca Miller Subscriber
Published October 22, 2026
17
While I sympathize with the desire for smaller districts, as a fiscal conservative I worry about the practical cost of adding 150 or 200 new congressional offices. Every single House member comes with a staff budget, franking privileges, security details, and pension obligations. Before we grow the size of Congress, shouldn't we require them to balance the federal budget and rein in unelected administrative agencies first? Adding more politicians in Washington won't automatically shrink the federal government.

Impact on the Electoral College and Rural States

Sarah Jenkins ✓ Verified Member
Published October 25, 2026
31
One constitutional detail every conservative should examine is how expanding the House directly affects the Electoral College. Because each state's electoral votes equal its House seats plus two Senate seats, increasing the House reduces the relative weight of the two-senator boost that protects smaller, rural states. However, it would also break up massive winner-take-all urban blocks and give suburban and rural counties inside larger states far stronger representation in both Congress and presidential elections. It is a constitutional trade-off worth careful prayer and study.
